📄 Read the script

How long can the financing of the AI boom hold up when even OpenAI lowers its funding target by ten billion dollars?

On its own, the report is no catastrophe, but it fits a pattern. The sell-off in US tech stocks is sharpening investor worries about the enormous spending on AI. At the same time, Alphabet's shares fell on high spending, and at Amazon AI investment is weighing on the stock even though the cloud business keeps growing. The market is no longer asking whether AI gets built, but who pays for it.

The cracks also show up in infrastructure. Large companies are pouring billions into compute capacity, yet debt, negative cash flow and skepticism are rising. For Anthropic, Blackstone forecasts a rating in high junk territory after an IPO, which would make raising capital costlier. It is only a prediction, but it makes clear that even leading model providers are financially vulnerable.

What does this mean for companies that use AI? Anyone who builds long term on a single provider takes on that provider's financing risk. One move in the right direction is the growing openness of tools. Google now allows models from Anthropic and OpenAI in Android Studio, so teams can pick the model that fits the task and the budget. That reduces dependency, at least inside the development environment.

Infrastructure also has a physical and legal side. In Finland, around 250,000 trees were illegally felled for a Google data center, and authorities halted work at two sites. In the United States, a court is letting a lawsuit against xAI over data center emissions proceed. On top of that, the Justice Department is examining whether Nvidia's purchase of Hugging Face harms competition. Anyone expanding AI capacity runs into environmental rules and antitrust law, and both cost time.

Meanwhile a less visible problem is growing. An Anthropic study shows that experts adopt AI recommendations in 86 percent of cases, often unconsciously. Human oversight is therefore not automatically independent review. When agents start triggering business processes, as Oracle plans in Fusion, the cross-check has to be built into the process, not merely drawn on an org chart.

Security is shifting too. Claude found an eleven-year-old flaw in the Linux kernel that allows remote code execution. At the same time, the official Claude Code extension in the Cursor marketplace contained an SSH backdoor. AI serves defense and offense alike, and developers' own tools become targets. Extensions are software dependencies and should be treated as such.

That leaves the question of people. Because AI takes over tasks, companies hire fewer entry-level staff, which pays off in the short term and opens a gap in experienced developers later. Experience grows only where someone has beginner years. Here is the dry punchline of the week: we save the apprenticeship today and desperately search for the master in ten years.

The core message of the week is that AI is not failing on technology but on capital, control and talent, and companies do well to put all three into their planning.
